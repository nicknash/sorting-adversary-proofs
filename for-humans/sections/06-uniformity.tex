\section{One adversary with a fixed polynomial time bound}
\label{sec:uniformity}

A family of fixed-stage algorithms attaining coefficients approaching one
would not by itself prove Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. We must choose a stage
effectively from $n$, with one polynomial exponent for all input sizes.

\subsection{An explicit sequence of stages}

For integers $j\geq4$, choose
\[
             D=j+4,\qquad H=2^j,\qquad \eta=\frac1{100j}.
\]
Compute $d,h,B$ from \eqref{eq:base-parameters}, then set
\[
 \sigma=1+2B,\qquad J=2^{j\sigma+1},\qquad
 L=\max\{4,\lceil C_*/\eta\rceil\}.
\]
Compute $A,R$ from \eqref{eq:scale-parameters}, compute $W_0,\rho$
using Appendix~\ref{app:local}, and put $G=4^B W_0$.
Each stage is a finite, explicitly computable list of integers and rationals.

For $q\leq2T$, Lemma~\ref{lem:rare} gives
\begin{equation}
             X\leq\frac{6T}{j},\qquad
             \sigma\Sc\leq\frac{T}{j},\qquad
             n+T+\Mov\leq6T.
\label{eq:stage-errors}
\end{equation}
Monotonicity of the last term of \eqref{eq:master} therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 40X\logtwo\left(2+\frac{n+T+\Mov}{X}\right)
           \leq\frac{240\logtwo(j+2)}j\,T.
\label{eq:stage-expensive}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Size caps and bounded setup}

At initialization, spend at most $n$ bit-computation steps tabulating
consecutive stages. Never use a partially computed stage.
Accept a completed stage only when
\begin{equation}
 G^2\leq\lfloor\logtwo(n+1)\rfloor,\qquad
 2(d+1)^{\rho+1}\leq\lfloor\logtwo(n+1)\rfloor.
\label{eq:caps}
\end{equation}
Use the largest accepted stage, or the fixed-order fallback if there is none.
Logarithmic upper roundings in the stage formulas can be computed using
integer bit lengths. The tabulation has a literal step budget: very large
intermediate computations may be interrupted without using their partial
results. Thus setup itself has a uniform polynomial bound.

Every fixed stage takes finitely many steps and has finite constants.
For all sufficiently large $n$ it is both completed and accepted.
Consequently the selected stage $j(n)$ tends to infinity.
The square on $G$ is needed:
\[
               \frac{3nG}{T}
               \leq\frac{3\sqrt{\logtwo(n+1)}}{\logtwo n}
               \longrightarrow0.
\]

\subsection{Bit complexity}

The second cap bounds the union of two query neighborhoods by
$b\leq\logtwo(n+1)$ vertices. A node has at most $2n+2$ positions.
The counter \eqref{eq:dp} therefore has at most
$K3^b\leq(2n+2)(n+1)^{\logtwo3}$ transitions, with a polynomial factor
in $b$ for their tests. Counts have $O(b\log K)=O(\log^2(n+1))$
bits. Scanning all positions to locate the approximate quartiles is still
polynomial, as are exact rational comparisons of the resulting probabilities.

Reachability, interval propagation, components, and batch manipulation use
polynomial-size data. Longest paths in an acyclic directed graph are found
by dynamic programming in a topological order; no enumeration of paths of
length $H$ is required. A repair removes an edge or a free item and does
not initiate a child repair. Before the query-count cutoff of
Section~\ref{sec:answer}, Lemma~\ref{lem:population} bounds the number of
moves, nodes, and stored comparisons polynomially in $n$. Afterwards a
fixed total order suffices. These bounds give a single polynomial number
of bit operations per answer.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Legality follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:routing}, and the preceding argument
proves the time bound. For the comparison guarantee assume $q\leq2T$,
since the other case is immediate. Divide \eqref{eq:master} by $T$.
The terminal remainder is $O(\log\log n/\log n)$; the cheap remainder
tends to zero by \eqref{eq:caps}; and
\eqref{eq:stage-errors}--\eqref{eq:stage-expensive} make the other repair
remainders $O(\log(j(n)+2)/j(n))$. Also $\eta\to0$.
Thus $T\leq(1+o(1))q+o(T)$, proving $q\geq(1-o(1))n\logtwo n$.
\end{proof}

The argument also applies to each fixed sequence of random choices of an
always-correct sorter. This is a statement about interaction with an
adaptive adversary; it does not assert a single fixed hard input
independent of all possible random choices.
